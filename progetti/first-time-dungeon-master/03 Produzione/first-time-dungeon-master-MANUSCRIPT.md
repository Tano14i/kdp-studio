## How to use this book (and the templates)

You already know how to play. You've rolled the dice, made a character, argued about
initiative order. Now you're on the other side of the screen, and it's a different
job. This book teaches that job, and only that job. It does not re-explain what a d20
is or how a saving throw works. You have that already. If you don't, one read of the
starter rules covers it, and then you come back here.

Every chapter answers one problem a first-time DM has, in the order you hit it: your
first session, fast prep, session zero, rulings, improvisation, NPCs, encounters,
locations, pacing, table trouble, and turning one night into a campaign. Two later
chapters cover playing online and handing out treasure, and the appendices give you two
finished adventures, a starter bestiary, and tables to build your own. Read it front to
back once. After that, treat it as a toolbox. Open the chapter for the problem in front
of you.

The spine of the book is the templates. Each chapter ends with a fillable sheet that
turns what you just read into something you can use at the table tonight. Copy them,
print them, scribble on them. A blank, printable pack of every template in this book
is waiting at the link on the last page. The book teaches the method. The templates
carry it into your game.

One promise before you start: you can run a good first session without being a good
DM yet. Confidence at the table is a set of skills, and skills are built by doing, not
by reading one more chapter. This book gets you doing fast.

Turn the page. Your first session starts now.


## Chapter 1: Your First Session Starts Now

Your group is looking at you. The snacks are out, the dice are on the table, and
someone says, "Okay, so what do we do?" That moment is the whole job. Not the lore
you wrote, not the map you drew. The moment you say the first sentence and the game
begins.

Most first-time DMs try to be ready for everything before that moment. They read the
whole rulebook, build a world with five kingdoms, and stat out a villain the players
will not meet for a year. Then session one arrives and none of it helps, because the
players walk left when the notes went right. You do not need to be ready for
everything. You need five things. This chapter is those five things, and nothing else.

### The five things you need

**A place to start, and a reason to care.** Your players need somewhere to be and a
problem in front of them on the first line. A tavern is not a start. A tavern with a
frightened messenger who dies before he finishes his sentence is a start. Give them a
scene with trouble already in it.

**A goal for tonight.** One clear thing the players could do in this session. Find the
missing child. Clear the cellar. Reach the next town alive. It does not have to be
what they end up doing. It gives the night a shape and gives you something to steer
back toward when the table drifts.

**Three names and three faces.** Someone will ask "who's the innkeeper?" and you will
freeze. Beat that now. Have three names ready and give each person one thing you can
play: a stutter, a grudge, a limp. That is enough to make an NPC (a non-player
character, anyone the players talk to who isn't a player) feel real.

**One thing that can go wrong.** A fight, a trap, a betrayal. One planned moment of
danger gives the session a spike of tension and gives the players something to beat.
You will build encounters properly in Chapter 7. For tonight, one is enough.

**A way to say yes.** Your most-used tool is not a rule. It is the habit of taking
what a player tries and building on it instead of blocking it. "Can I check the body
for a letter?" Yes. Now there's a letter. You decide what it says. Saying yes keeps
the table moving and keeps the story theirs.

That is the list. Notice what is not on it. No world map. No pantheon. No twelve-step
backstory for the villain. Those are fun to make and they will not save your first
session. The five things will.

### Fear is the tax, not the wall

Here is the truth no one tells a new DM plainly. Yes, the players will do something
you did not plan. They will talk to the barrel, romance the guard, and ignore the door
you spent an hour describing. That is not a sign you failed to prepare. That is the
game working. A tabletop game the players cannot surprise is a book, not a game.

So expect the swerve and stop fearing it. When it comes, you fall back on the five
things and the habit of yes. The rest of this book gives you a tool for each kind of
swerve: a way to make a ruling on the spot, a table to invent an NPC in ten seconds, a
method to build a fight fast. You are not walking in unarmed. You are walking in with a
kit, and you are about to learn to use it.

### At a real table
Taka is running her first session for four friends. She skips the world map and fills
the five slots. The players start in a mill where the miller's daughter has just been
dragged into the river by something with too many arms. Tonight's goal is to get her
back. Her three NPCs are a shaking miller, a smug town reeve, and a hedge-witch who
knows the river. One danger: the thing in the water. When a player says "I jump in after
her," Taka does not reach for a rule. She says yes, sets the stakes, and calls for a
Strength check. Twenty minutes in, nobody has noticed she has no map, because the trouble
was on the first line and the table has been chasing it ever since.

> **At the table:** when your mind goes blank, ask the players a question. "What does
> your character do?" hands the moment back to them and buys you the three seconds you
> need to think. A DM who asks good questions looks more prepared than one who talks
> the most.

## Chapter 2: The One-Hour Prep System

It's Wednesday. You play Saturday. Between now and then you have a job, a life, and
a slow dread that says you're not ready.

Most first-time DMs fall into the same trap. They open the rulebook "to prepare"
and three hours later they've read about siege weapons, planar travel, and a table
for random tavern names, none of which their players will touch on Saturday. They
mistake reading for prep. Then Saturday comes, and they cancel.

You're not going to do that. You're going to prep the whole session in one hour,
using the same six steps every time. Set a timer. When the hour is up, you stop.
A session prepped in an hour and run with confidence beats a "perfect" session that
never happens.

One rule before we start: **prep the first fifteen minutes of play in detail, and
sketch the rest.** Players are unpredictable. The opening scene is the only part
you control, so make it sharp. Everything after that, you improvise from a few
strong notes. That's the skill the rest of this book builds.

### The six steps (and the clock)

Each step has a time budget. The budget matters more than the polish. If a step
runs long, cut it off and move on. You can always add detail later. You can
never get Saturday back.

**Step 1. The strong open (15 minutes).**
Write the first scene as if it were the first page of a movie. Where are the
players? What's happening the moment you say "we begin"? Give them something to
react to on line one: a scream down the hall, or a stranger who already knows
their names. Not "you're all in a tavern." A tavern is a place. You need an event.
Write three sentences: the setup, the trouble, and the choice the players face.
That's your open. Fifteen minutes, done.

**Step 2. The want (5 minutes).**
Name what your villain or problem *wants*, in one sentence. Not their backstory.
Their want. "The cult wants to wake the thing under the lake." A want gives every
scene a direction, because the world keeps moving toward it whether the players act
or not. If you know the want, you can improvise the rest.

**Step 3. Three beats (10 minutes).**
Sketch three things that could happen, in the loosest possible form. A place they
might go, a person they might meet, a fight they might pick. Three bullet points, a
line each. Do not script them. These are ingredients, not a plot. If the players
skip all three, you've lost ten minutes, not ten hours.

**Step 4. One fight, roughed in (15 minutes).**
You'll likely have one combat. Pick the monsters now so you're not flipping pages
mid-game. Write down their names, how many, and where the fight happens. Don't do
the math on balance yet. Chapter 7 gives you a formula for that in ten minutes. For
now, choose, and note the page number where each monster's stats live.

**Step 5. Names and faces (10 minutes).**
The one thing that will catch you flat-footed is a player asking a nameless
shopkeeper, "So who are you?" Prep kills that panic. Write five names now. Give
three of them a single quirk: a limp, a nervous laugh, a grudge. When a player talks
to someone you didn't plan, you grab a name off the list and you're never caught
empty.

**Step 6. The safety net (5 minutes).**
Write down three things you always forget in the moment: where you left the last
session, what the players said they'd do next, and one reward they can find if they
get stuck. This is the note you'll reach for during play.

Add it up: fifteen, five, ten, fifteen, ten, five. One hour. Stop when it's done.

### At a real table
It is 8:40 on a Tuesday and Marcus has twenty minutes before bed. He sets a timer and
runs the six steps. Strong open: bandits have strung a rope across the forest road and
are robbing a merchant when the party rides up. The want: the bandit chief needs coin to
pay off a worse enemy. Then three beats, one fight roughed in, five names, a safety-net
note. The timer beeps at nine. He closes the notebook with a session ready and his
evening back. On Saturday the players ignore the merchant entirely and chase the bandits
home, and it does not matter, because Marcus prepped a situation, not a script.

> **At the table:** keep the safety-net note (Step 6) on top of your pile. When the
> game stalls and your mind goes blank, look there first. It's the fastest way back
> into the story.

### Why an hour is enough

New DMs over-prepare because prep feels safe and running the game feels scary. But
the players never see your prep. They see how you handle the table: your rulings,
your improvisation, the way you keep things moving. Those are table skills, not
paper skills, and no amount of extra prep buys them. Preparing less forces you to
build the skills that matter, and this book builds them with you.

There's a second reason. A session you scripted to the minute breaks the moment the
players go off-script. And they will, on line one. A session built from six strong
notes bends instead. You're not memorizing a play. You're packing a kit.

So next Wednesday, set the timer. Run the six steps. Close the book when the hour's
up, and go live your life until Saturday. You're readier than you think.

## Chapter 3: Session Zero Without the Awkwardness

Half of the problems that wreck a campaign are set on the first night, before a single
die is rolled. One player wants a grim war story. Another wants to make jokes and rob
shopkeepers. Someone assumed there would be no killing of good characters, and nobody
checked. None of them are wrong. They never said what they wanted out loud.

A session zero (the meeting before you play, where the table agrees on what the game
is) fixes this in one sitting. It is not a rules lecture and it should not feel like a
form to fill out. Done well, it takes forty minutes, gets everyone talking, and hands
you a map of what your players actually want. Here is how to run one without the room
going quiet and weird.

### Set the frame first

Start by saying what kind of game you are offering, in two sentences. "I'm thinking a
grounded fantasy about a frontier town in trouble. More problem-solving than dungeon-
crawling, some combat, room for your characters' pasts to matter." You are not asking
permission. You are giving the table something to react to, the same way you give the
players a scene with trouble already in it. A frame invites a yes or a "can we tweak
this," and both are useful.

Then hand the game to them with three questions. Ask each player: what does your
character want, what are they afraid of, and why would they leave home to do this? Keep
the answers short. You are not writing novels. You are collecting hooks, one per
player, that you will pull on all campaign.

### Agree on the table's edges

Every group has lines. Some tables are fine with horror and betrayal. Some are not.
Finding out mid-scene, when a player goes pale, is the worst possible time. So ask
now, plainly and without drama: is anything off the table for us? Common ones are harm
to children, graphic torture, and real-world bigotry played straight. Write down what
comes up. Nobody has to explain why. "That's a no for me" is a full sentence.

Add two practical agreements while you have everyone. How does the group make decisions
when they disagree, and what happens when someone has to miss a session. Deciding these
cold, in five calm minutes, saves you an ugly argument later when tempers are up.

### Tie their characters together

The single most common first-campaign failure is five strangers with no reason to stay
in the same room. Fix it before you play. Give the group two minutes to answer one
question together: how do at least two of you already know each other? A shared debt, a
sibling, a war they both survived. You do not need a web connecting everyone. You need
enough thread that the party is a party on line one, not a collection of loners who
happen to share a table.

Close by pointing at the hooks you collected. "Kesh, your character wants to find the
brother who vanished. That's going to matter early." You are telling each player their
choices were heard, and you are quietly promising a game built around them. That promise
is why they come back for session two.

### At a real table
Before his first campaign, Devi spends forty minutes on a session zero. He offers a
frame: a mining town where the deep tunnels have gone quiet. He asks each player the
three questions and learns one wants revenge, one wants to get rich and leave, one is
running from family. He asks about table edges, and a player quietly says no torture,
thank you. Devi writes it down without a word. Two characters turn out to be estranged
cousins. By the time dice come out, the party has a reason to be together and Devi has
four hooks he will pull for months. The campaign is built before it starts.

> **At the table:** keep your session-zero notes in front of you during play, not filed
> away. When the game sags, pull a player's stated fear or want into the scene. Nothing
> re-grabs a distracted table faster than making its own answers come true.

## Chapter 4: Rulings, Not Rules

A player says, "I want to swing on the chandelier and kick the guard off the balcony."
Everyone looks at you. There is no page for this. Your heart rate climbs, and some part
of you wants to flip through the book to find the swinging-on-chandeliers rule. There
isn't one. There does not need to be one. What the table needs is a decision, and you
are allowed to make it.

This is the fear that keeps new DMs up at night: getting a rule wrong in front of
people who know the game better than you. Here is the reframe that ends it. Your job is
not to remember every rule. Your job is to make a fair, fast ruling and keep the game
moving. A ruling is a call you make on the spot when the rules are silent, unclear, or
too slow to look up. Good DMs make dozens a night. Nobody at the table can tell the
difference between a rule you knew and a ruling you invented, as long as it was fair.

### The four-step ruling

When you hit a moment the rules do not cover, run these four steps in your head. They
take about five seconds with practice.

**Ask what they're really trying to do.** Not the stunt, the goal. Swinging on the
chandelier is the how. Knocking the guard off the balcony is the what. Rule on the
what. This one question dissolves most rules confusion, because a clear goal usually
points at a clear check.

**Pick the obvious check.** Match the goal to an ability the player already has on
their sheet. A daring physical stunt is Strength or Dexterity. Talking someone down is
Charisma. Noticing a lie is Wisdom. You are not finding the "correct" check. You are
finding a reasonable one. Reasonable is enough.

**Set the stakes before the roll.** Say what happens on a success and what happens on a
failure, out loud, before dice hit the table. "Hit it and the guard goes over the rail.
Miss and you're hanging there, exposed, and he gets a free swing at you." Now the roll
means something and the player chose it with eyes open.

**Let the dice land and move on.** Whatever the roll says, narrate it and push forward.
Do not reopen the ruling to check whether you did it "right." A ruling made and honored
beats a rule looked up over three quiet minutes while the table checks their phones.

### When a player says you got it wrong

Sometimes the rules lawyer at your table is correct, and says so mid-scene. Have one
response ready: "Good catch. We'll use the real rule from here. For this roll, we'll
keep what we said." This does three things. It thanks them, it fixes the future, and it
protects the flow of the current moment. Say it once and mean it, and even the most
rules-focused player will settle, because you gave their knowledge a place to go.

Never rule to punish a clever idea. If a player finds a way to make a fight easier or
skip a trap you were proud of, that is not cheating. That is the game working. Reward
the cleverness and build the next challenge harder. A table learns fast whether its DM
says yes to good ideas or looks for reasons to say no, and that lesson decides how bold
your players will be for the rest of the campaign.

### At a real table
A player wants to tip a bookshelf onto a charging cultist. There is no bookshelf-tipping
rule. Priya runs the four steps in her head. The goal is to stop the charge, Strength
fits, and she says the stakes out loud. "Beat a 15 and he's pinned and prone. Miss and
you're off balance, and he gets there first." The player rolls a 17, the shelf comes
down, the table cheers. Later a rules-focused player mentions the official way to handle
improvised attacks. "Good catch," Priya says. "Real rule from here. This one stands."
Nobody could tell she invented the whole thing on the spot.

> **At the table:** when in doubt, make a ruling and add "we'll confirm after the
> session." It keeps the game moving now and gives you a clean way to look up the real
> rule later, without a five-minute stall while everyone waits on you.

## Chapter 5: Confident Improvisation

The players just walked into a shop you never planned, to ask a shopkeeper who does not
exist about a rumor you never wrote. This is the moment every new DM dreads, and it
happens every single session. You cannot prep your way out of it. There are infinite
things five creative people might do, and you will never guess them all. So you stop
trying to guess, and you learn to build on the spot instead.

Improvisation is not a gift some DMs are born with. It is a set of small moves and a few
tables you can lean on until the moves become instinct. This chapter gives you both. By
the end you will trust that whatever the players throw at you, you can catch it and
throw something back.

### The one habit: yes, and

Improv comedy runs on two words, and they work at a game table too. When a player
offers something, take it and add to it. "Is there a back door?" Yes, and it's chained
from the outside, which is strange. "Do I know this guard?" Yes, and you owe him money.
The player hands you a gift every time they ask a question or try an action. Your job is
to accept it and raise. Blocking, the habit of answering "no, there's nothing," starves
the scene and teaches players to stop trying.

There is a cousin move for when yes would break your story: yes, but. "Can I persuade
the king to send his army?" Yes, but he wants your character's family name as
collateral. The players still get a path forward, and you get a complication that makes
the next scene. Between yes and, and yes but, you almost never have to say a flat no.

### When your mind goes blank, roll

Nobody improvises well under a spotlight with an empty head. That is what the tables
below are for. When a player asks for something you did not prepare, do not scramble.
Roll a d20 (the twenty-sided die) on the matching table, read the result, and say it
with confidence. The players will never know you did not plan it. Over time you will
stop needing the tables, because you will have internalized the range of answers they
hold. Until then, they are your net.

Use them live. Keep them where you can see them. And feel free to swap any entry for one
that fits your world better. The point is to never sit in silence.

### At a real table
The party strolls into a temple Sam never wrote and asks the priest about a stolen

incomplete," and has Ysolde send them to the wrong suspect in complete good faith. The

> **At the table:** repeat the player's idea back before you answer it. "You want to

When an improvised NPC or complication lands well, write it down after the session.
Bren the nervous clerk who was supposed to appear once becomes a recurring favorite
because you kept the note. Improvisation feeds your prep, and your prep frees you to
improvise. That loop, not a thicker binder, is what makes a confident DM.

You can now catch anything the players throw. Next you'll make the people they meet

## Chapter 6: NPCs Your Players Remember

Ask any group about their favorite campaign moment and half the time it is a person, not
a battle. The paranoid shopkeeper. The knight who quoted bad poetry. The villain who was
almost sympathetic. Players remember characters. And here is the good news for a new DM:
a memorable NPC takes one line, not one page. The DMs who write three paragraphs of
backstory are doing more work for a worse result.

An NPC (a non-player character, anyone the players talk to who isn't a player) needs
exactly three things to come alive at the table: a want, a trait, and a voice you can
do. Backstory is optional and mostly for you. What the players feel is those three
things, delivered in the moment. This chapter makes each of them fast.

### A want, so they push

Every NPC on stage should want something in the scene, even something small. The
merchant wants to close before dark. The guard wants to avoid trouble on his watch. The
want gives you a way to play the NPC without a script, because you always know what they
are trying to get. When a player talks to them, you have the NPC pursue the want,
and the scene writes itself. An NPC with no want stands there like furniture and the
conversation dies.

### A trait, so they stick

One vivid, playable trait is what the players remember. Not a list. One. A twitchy eye,
a habit of flattering everyone, a refusal to make eye contact, a laugh at the wrong
moments. Pick something you can show through action and speech, not something you have to
narrate. "He keeps glancing at the door" tells the players more than "he is anxious," and
it gives them something to poke at.

### A voice you can do

You do not need to be an actor. You need contrast, so the players can tell one NPC from
another. Change one dial: talk faster, drop to a near-whisper, clip your sentences short,
add a verbal tic they repeat. A single consistent choice reads as a full character. If a
voice feels like too much in front of your group, use posture and phrasing instead. The
blunt laborer says "what do you want" while the oily official says "ah, how may I be of
service." Same information, different person.

### Reuse without the players noticing

You will not invent every NPC fresh. Good DMs keep a short stable of people they can drop
into any town: a fixer, a healer, a bully, a source of rumors. When the players need a
face you did not plan, you pull one from the stable, rename it, and adjust one trait. The
missing-child plot in this town and the smuggling plot in the next can both use "the
tired guard who wants an easy shift," and no one will ever know it was the same sketch.

### At a real table
The party needs a smith. Lin does not narrate a biography. She gives him a want (close
early, his kid is sick), a trait (wipes his hands on the same rag over and over), and one
dial (clipped, tired sentences). "Make it quick. What do you need." A player mentions the
sick child they overheard about, and Lin has the smith soften, a little. Three sessions
later the players still call him "the rag guy" and go out of their way to help him. He
took one line to build and he outlived half the plot Lin actually planned.

> **At the table:** give the NPC an opinion about the players before they speak. A
> stranger who already likes or distrusts the party turns a flat "how can I help you"
> into a scene with tension in it, and it costs you nothing to decide on the spot.

### Write the one line, not the page

The card below is the whole method. Fill it in one line per NPC for the handful of people
you know the players will meet, and leave blanks to fill live with the tables from
Chapter 5 for everyone else. A stack of these one-line cards is worth more than a
document of NPC biographies you will never open at the table.

```

WANT (in this scene): _______________________________
TRAIT (one, playable): ______________________________
VOICE (one dial to turn): ___________________________
   ⃝ faster   ⃝ whisper   ⃝ clipped   ⃝ verbal tic: ______

OPINION OF THE PARTY:  likes __ / neutral __ / distrusts __

something to fight over, and learn to build a balanced encounter in ten minutes.


## Chapter 7: Build an Encounter in Ten Minutes

Two fights go wrong for new DMs, and they are opposites. In the first, the monsters fall
over in one round and the players shrug. In the second, a random pack of wolves nearly
kills the whole party and everyone goes quiet. Both come from the same source: guessing
at difficulty instead of measuring it. You do not need deep math to measure it. You need
a rough budget and a danger dial, and you can set both in ten minutes.

Ignore the temptation to calculate exact challenge ratings mid-prep. The official math
is fine and slow. What follows is a fast heuristic that lands close enough to run a
tense, fair fight, which is all a first-time DM needs. Tune it up over a few sessions as
you learn your group.

### The budget: threat points

Give your party a threat budget based on its size and level. Then spend that budget on
monsters. Roughly, one "threat point" is a monster whose level (its challenge rating, the
game's difficulty number for a monster, written CR) equals your players' level. A CR-2
monster against level-2 heroes is one point. Two of them is two points.

Here is the whole rule. For a standard fight, the budget equals the number of players.
Four players, four points, and you spend it on monsters near their level. Want a hard
fight? Spend one and a half times the players. Want an easy warm-up? Spend half. That is
the entire formula, and it is close enough.

The trick with the budget is spread. Four CR-1 monsters and one CR-4 monster both cost
about four points against a level-4 party, but they play completely differently. Many
weak monsters threaten to overwhelm and swing wildly. One big monster is predictable and
can be focused down. Mixing a mid-size threat with a couple of minions gives you the best
fights, because the players have choices about what to hit first.

### The danger dial: three levers

The budget sets the size of the fight. Three levers set how dangerous it feels, and you
turn them at the table without touching the numbers.

**Terrain.** A flat empty room is the least interesting fight in the game. Add one
feature the players and monsters both use: a chasm to shove enemies into, cover to hide
behind, a fire spreading each round. Terrain does more for tension than an extra monster.

**Numbers over time.** A fight that starts hard and stays hard is exhausting. A fight
where two more enemies arrive on round three, unless the players do something about the
alarm bell, has a built-in clock and a built-in choice. Reinforcements are a dial you can
turn up or down live, based on how the party is holding.

**An exit.** Players fight harder and panic less when losing does not mean dying. Give
most fights a way out: a door to flee through, a foe who takes prisoners, a chance to
surrender. Save the no-escape fight for a moment you have built toward, and it will land
much harder for being rare.

### At a real table
Four players at level three walk into an ambush. Reese sets a standard budget of four
points: one CR-2 bandit captain and four weak thugs, spread so the captain is the real
threat. He turns one danger lever, a rain-slick bridge both sides can shove enemies off.
The fight swings when a thug goes over the rail and the captain, seeing it turn, tries to
flee. Reese lets him, because he built an exit. Nobody dies, everyone burned resources,
and the captain escapes to become next month's problem. A fair fight, built in ten
minutes, that fed the campaign.

> **At the table:** watch the party's resources, not their hit points, to judge a fight.
> If they are winning but burning spells and potions fast, the fight is doing its job.
> Attrition across a session is scarier and fairer than one deadly encounter that can
> end a character to a bad roll.

### Rough it in, then run it

The worksheet below is what "ten minutes" looks like. Set the budget, pick a spread of
monsters, turn one or two danger levers, and note where the stat blocks live so you are
not flipping pages mid-fight. You are not designing a perfect encounter. You are building
a fair one fast, and leaving yourself room to adjust while it plays.

```

## Chapter 8: Dungeons and Locations on Demand

A player says, "We go down into the old crypt." You had a name and nothing else. Now you
need a place, with rooms and danger and a reason to go in, and you need it in the next
thirty seconds. New DMs freeze here because they think a dungeon is a hand-drawn map with
forty numbered rooms. It is not. A dungeon is five rooms with a point, and you can build
one at the table.

This chapter gives you a template that fits almost any location the players enter: a
crypt, a bandit fort, a wizard's tower, a haunted mansion. Same five beats, different
paint. Learn it once and you will never be caught empty when the party opens a door you
did not plan.

### The five-room shape

A satisfying location has a shape, not only rooms in a row. This one has five beats, and
they can bend around whatever map you sketch.

**The entrance, with a guardian.** The way in should cost something: a lock, a lookout, a
puzzle, a warning. It tells the players they have crossed into danger and sets the tone.

**The puzzle or roleplay challenge.** A room that is not solved by fighting. A riddle, a
trapped hall, a prisoner who will talk, a choice between two doors. This is where the
players who do not love combat get their moment.

**The setback.** Something goes wrong here. A trap they trip, an ambush, the floor gives
way, a betrayal. The setback drains resources and raises the stakes before the climax.

**The climax.** The reason the place exists. The boss, the sealed vault, the ritual about
to complete. Everything before this was building toward it, and the players should feel
that.

**The reward and the twist.** They win, and they get something: treasure, an ally, the
information they came for. Attach one twist that points at what comes next. The stolen
ledger names a bigger villain. The freed prisoner has a warning. The reward opens the
next adventure.

### Paint it in one pass

Once you know the five beats, you dress them. Pick a theme, then ask what each beat looks
like in that theme. A crypt's guardian is a sealed stone door and a warning carved in old
script. A bandit fort's guardian is a lookout on the wall. Same beat, different paint. You
can do this out loud while the players describe their approach, and it will feel authored,
because the shape underneath is doing the work.

You do not need a grid map for most locations. A rough sketch of five connected spaces,
or even a list of five rooms, runs fine as long as you can tell the players what they see
and what their choices are. Draw a proper map only when the exact geometry matters, like a
fight where positioning is the whole point.

### At a real table
"We go into the old lighthouse," a player says, and Noor has a name and nothing else. She
fills the five beats live. Entrance: a rusted door chained from inside, so someone is
here. Puzzle: a room of mirrors that must be turned toward the sea. Setback: the stairs
collapse behind them. Climax: a smuggler-mage finishing a signal-spell at the top. Reward
and twist: the treasure is a ledger naming a councilwoman the party trusts. Noor drew
nothing but a stack of five boxes on scrap paper, and the players will talk about the
lighthouse for weeks.

> **At the table:** describe every new room in three quick strokes: what you see, what you
> hear or smell, and the one thing that invites action. "Cold water to your ankles, a drip
> echoing somewhere ahead, and a rusted grate in the far wall that's been pried open
> recently." Three strokes give a room life without a paragraph.

### Build the crawl in five lines

The sheet below turns a location name into a runnable dungeon in the time it takes the
party to light a torch. Fill the five beats, add a theme, and you have a place with a
point. For locations you know the players will visit, prep one ahead. For the ones they
surprise you with, fill it live, one beat at a time as they move.

### The 5-Room Dungeon Sheet

One per location. Printable copy in the download pack (last page).

```

      The cost to get in: ______________________________
2 · PUZZLE / ROLEPLAY CHALLENGE
      Solved without fighting by: ______________________

      What goes wrong: _________________________________

      The reason this place exists: ____________________

```

Your party can open any door and find a place ready for them. Next you'll keep the story
moving between those places, so the game never stalls out.


## Chapter 9: Keep the Story Moving

There is a specific silence every new DM learns to dread. The players have finished a
scene, they look at each other, and someone says, "So... what do we do now?" Nobody knows
where to go. The energy leaks out of the room. You built a world and the party is standing
in it, lost. This is a pacing problem, and pacing is a skill you can learn as cleanly as
any rule.

Two failures cause most stalls. Either the players do not know what they could do next, or
they know but cannot find the way. This chapter fixes both, and it does it without
railroading, which is the trap on the other side: dragging players down the one path you
prepared and punishing them for stepping off it. The goal is a game that flows and still
belongs to the players.

### Never hide the next step behind one roll

The most common stall is a mystery gated behind a single check. The clue is in the study,
the players roll to search, they fail, and now the story cannot continue because the
information is trapped behind a die. Avoid this with a simple habit: any fact the plot
needs, the players find automatically. Rolls decide how much extra they get, not whether
the story moves.

The clean version of this is the three-clue rule. For any conclusion you need the players
to reach, plant three separate paths to it. Three witnesses, three pieces of evidence,
three ways to learn the cult meets at midnight. Players are inventive and will miss two of
anything. With three, they almost always find one, and they feel clever instead of stuck,
because they think they solved it rather than followed your rail.

### When they stall anyway, apply pressure

Sometimes the party has the information and still spins in place, debating options in a
town while the game idles. Do not wait them out. Make the world act. A clock the villain
is racing gives every idle minute a cost. A messenger arrives with news that forces a
choice. The thing they were deciding whether to investigate happens to someone else. When
the players will not move the story, move the story at them, and watch the table snap back
to life.

Pressure is also how you handle the party that scatters to do five things at once. Instead
of tracking five threads, tell them the world will not wait: the festival is tonight, the
caravan leaves at dawn, the ritual completes at the new moon. A shared deadline pulls a
scattered party back into one story without you forcing them onto a path.

### Give every scene a turn

A scene that ends where it began is a scene that saps momentum. Aim for each one to change
something: the players learn a fact, gain or lose an ally, or the situation gets worse. If
you notice a scene is only the players confirming what they already knew, cut to the next
thing. You are allowed to say "an hour passes, and then" and skip the boring part. New DMs
narrate every step. Confident DMs cut to where the choices are.

### At a real table
The party stalls, arguing in the market about whether the disappearances are worth their
time. Ari does not wait. He starts the clock he prepped: a child screams two streets
over, and by the time they arrive, another person is gone. The debate is over. Earlier
Ari made sure the key fact, that the cult meets at midnight, had three paths to it: a
witness, a dropped token, and a pattern in the dates. The party found the token, missed
the other two, and felt like detectives. The story moved, and they never saw the rails,
because there were three of them.

> **At the table:** end each session on a question, not a rest. Stop the moment a door
> creaks open, a letter arrives, or a stranger says their name. A cliffhanger does two
> jobs: it makes the group hungry for next time, and it hands you an obvious opening scene
> for your one-hour prep.

### Map the path, keep it open

The one-page tool below is not a plot you force. It is a safety net that guarantees the
players can always find a next step and always feel the pressure of a moving world. Fill
the clues so no fact is trapped behind one roll, set a clock so idling has a cost, and note
where you can cut when a scene runs flat.

One per adventure or arc. Printable copy in the download pack (last page).

```

THE THREE-CLUE RULE (the key fact the players must reach)
   Key fact: _______________________________________
   Clue path 1: ____________________________________
   Clue path 2: ____________________________________
   Clue path 3: ____________________________________

   The villain/world is racing to: _________________
   What happens if the party waits: ________________

   • ______________________________________________

Your game will flow and still belong to the players. Next you'll handle the moments the

## Chapter 10: When Things Go Wrong at the Table

Not every problem is in the story. Some are in the room. One player argues every ruling.
Another checks their phone through the whole session. Two of them keep talking over the
quiet one, who has not spoken in an hour. These are the problems no rulebook covers, and
they are the ones most likely to end a campaign, because a bad rules call is forgotten by
next week and a bad table feeling is not.

The good news is that table problems respond to the same tools as story problems: name
the thing, address it early, and keep it kind. You are not a boss and your players are not
staff. You are the person who volunteered to run the game, which gives you the right to
protect it. Here are the situations you will meet, and what to do.

### The rules lawyer

The player who knows the rules better than you and says so is usually not trying to
undermine you. They love the game and want it played right. Use that. Make them an ally:
"You know this better than me. Keep the real rule in your back pocket, and if I miss one,
flag it after the scene." You gave their knowledge a job and a time, which is all most
rules lawyers want. If one keeps stopping play to argue, hold the line you learned in
Chapter 4: the ruling stands for this roll, the real rule starts next time, and we move on
now.

### The disengaged player

Before you blame the player scrolling their phone, ask why. Often they are bored because
the spotlight has not touched them in an hour. The fix is on your side of the screen: turn
to them by name and hand them a moment. "The guard is blocking the door and looking right
at you. What do you do?" A direct question with their name on it pulls almost anyone back.
If it keeps happening across sessions, ask them privately what they want more of. Some
players want combat, some want intrigue, and a quiet one is often a player in the wrong
kind of scene, not a player who stopped caring.

### The spotlight hog and the quiet one

These two problems are the same problem, and you solve them together. One or two loud
players fill every scene while the quiet ones fade. Do not scold the loud ones. Redirect.
Make a habit of going around the table by name, especially to the players who have not
spoken. "Before you answer, Kesh, what's your character doing while this happens?" Spread
the spotlight on purpose and the loud players still get their fun while the quiet ones get
a way in. Balance is your job, and it is done with small nudges every scene, not one big
correction.

### Real conflict between players

Sometimes it is not a quirk, it is a clash: two players who want opposite games,
or a personal friction that spills onto the table. Do not try to solve this mid-session in
front of everyone. Pause the game if you must, then take it to a private conversation
after. Name what you saw without taking a side, and ask what would make the game better for
them. Most table conflict shrinks fast once someone with authority says out loud that they
noticed and they care.

### At a real table
Two sessions in, Jonah notices Priya has not spoken in forty minutes while two louder
players run every scene. He does not scold anyone. He starts going around the table by
name. "Before the guard answers, Priya, what's your character watching for?" She lights
up and spots the ambush the loud players walked past. Later, privately, Jonah asks a
checked-out player what they want more of. "Honestly, more fighting." The next session
opens with a brawl in the first ten minutes, and the player is back. Both problems fixed
with small nudges, no confrontation.

> **At the table:** the strongest tool you have is a check-in. Every few sessions, spend
> two minutes asking "what do you want more of, and less of?" It surfaces small
> frustrations before they grow into someone quietly quitting, and it tells the group you
> are running the game for them, not at them.

### Handle it with the sheet

The flowchart below is your calm-in-the-moment reference for the table problems that catch
new DMs off guard. It is not something you fill before play. Keep it nearby for the first
few sessions, glance at it when the room feels off, and address the problem the same night
in the smallest way that works.

```

## Chapter 11: From One Session to a Campaign

You ran the first session. The table laughed, someone almost died, and as everyone packed
up a player asked the best question a DM can hear: "So when's next time?" Now you have a
new job, and it is easier than the first one. A campaign is not eleven times the work of a
session. It is one session, then another, connected by a thin thread you tend between
games. This chapter shows you how to tend it without drowning in prep.

The mistake that ends first campaigns is not too little planning. It is too much. New DMs
try to map a year of story, the players ignore the plan in week two, and the DM feels like
a failure and quits. Do the opposite. Plan lightly, react fast, and let the campaign grow
out of what the players actually do.

### Close every session on purpose

The last five minutes of a session set up the next one, so do not let them dribble away.
Do two things before everyone leaves. End on a hook, the cliffhanger you learned in Chapter
9, so the group leaves hungry. Then take one minute to jot what just happened: what the
party did, what they said they would do next, and any thread you left dangling. That note is
tomorrow's prep, half-written, while it is still fresh.

### Prep the next session, not the whole story

Between games, you do the one-hour prep from Chapter 2, aimed at what the players pointed
at when they left. You do not prepare the branches they might take. You prepare the branch
they said they would take, and you keep the improv tables from Chapter 5 ready for the rest.
This is the whole rhythm of running a campaign: a light note at the end of each session, one
hour of focused prep before the next, and trust in your tools for everything you did not see
coming.

### Let the thread thicken slowly

A campaign gains depth by remembering, not by planning. The bandit the players spared in
session two comes back grateful in session six. The offhand rumor about the lake becomes the
next arc because the players kept poking at it. Your job between sessions is to look at your
notes and ask one question: what did the players care about, and how does the world respond?
Follow their attention and the campaign writes itself, one callback at a time. The villain
you planned matters less than the enemy they made by accident.

### Grow as a DM the same way

You will get better session by session, and the fastest way is to keep the same short note
for yourself that you keep for the story. After each game, write one thing that worked and
one thing you would change. Not a review, one line each. Over ten sessions those lines
become your own style guide, earned at the table instead of read in a book. The confident DM
you want to be is only you, a dozen sessions from now, having paid attention.

### At a real table
After session one, Bel spends sixty seconds on a recap: the party freed a prisoner,
promised to warn the next village, and left a bandit alive. Before the next game she runs
the one-hour prep on the village they are heading to, and she rewards their mercy: the
spared bandit turns up as a nervous informant. Ten sessions later, her "plan" is mostly
callbacks to things the players did, stitched together one recap at a time. She never
mapped a year of story. She only kept the note, and the campaign grew itself.

> **At the table:** keep a running "party legend" list where everyone can see it: names
> they've met, places they've been, promises they've made. It saves you from contradicting
> yourself, and it shows the players their choices are remembered, which is the feeling that
> turns a game group into a campaign.

### Carry it forward with two sheets

The templates below close the loop. The recap sheet captures a session in one minute while
it is fresh. The next-session sheet turns that recap into your one-hour prep. Run this loop
and a campaign stops being a mountain and becomes what it should be: the next good night,
and then the one after that.

## Chapter 12: Running the Game Online

Your table might not be a table. More first games happen over video and a virtual
tabletop now than around anyone's kitchen, and the online room has its own traps. The
good news is that every method in this book still works. You still prep in an hour, still
make rulings, still improvise with the tables. What changes is the plumbing, and a few
small habits keep the plumbing from eating your session.

This chapter is short on purpose. You do not need a streaming studio to run a good online
game. You need clear sound, a shared way to see the action, and a handful of moves that
replace the things a physical table gives you for free.

### Sound beats everything else

In person, people read each other without trying. Online, all of that collapses into
audio, so sound is the one thing worth getting right. Ask everyone to use headphones,
which kills the echo that makes a group unlistenable. A cheap wired headset beats a
laptop microphone in a hard room. Before you start, do one round of "say something" so you
catch the player whose mic is dead before they miss the first scene. Everything else you
can improvise. Bad audio you cannot.

### Give them one thing to look at

An online group drifts when there is nothing to share their eyes on. You do not need a
gridded battle map with animated torches. You need one shared screen: a simple map, a
picture of the room, even a document with the scene written on it. When combat starts,
something as plain as tokens on a grid keeps everyone clear on who is where. Pick one tool
and learn the three buttons you use. The fanciest virtual tabletop run by a DM
who is fighting the software is worse than a shared image and theater of the mind.

### Hand out the spotlight on purpose

The online room hides your quiet players even more than a real one does. Nobody leans in,
nobody catches an eye. So you do the work the table used to do. Go around by name every
scene, the same habit from Chapter 10, and it matters twice as much here. Watch for the
player who starts a sentence and gets talked over, and come back to them: "Kesh, you were
about to say something." Online, that small rescue is often the difference between a player
who stays and one who quietly stops showing up.

### Fight the second screen

Every player online is one click from a browser, a chat, a game. You will not win that
war by policing it. You win it by keeping scenes moving and stakes clear, so there is no
dead air to fill with a phone. End scenes a beat early. Cut to the next choice. Ask direct
questions that need an answer now. A tight online session holds attention that a slow one
loses, and the pacing tools from Chapter 9 are your best defense against the second screen.

> **At the table:** post the initiative order where everyone can see it and say "you're
> up, and Sela is on deck." Naming who is next keeps players present, because they know
> their moment is coming and they do not want to be caught scrolling when it arrives.

### At a real table

Dana runs her whole campaign over video for friends in three cities. Her prep is the same
one-hour system. Her only extra step is a two-minute sound check at the top and a single
shared map tab she updates by hand. When a fight breaks out she drops tokens on a grid and
narrates the rest. Her players forget they are not in the same room, because she spends her
attention on the spotlight and the pacing, not on software. The tools carried over. She
only changed the plumbing.

### Set up your virtual table

The checklist below is your pre-game plumbing pass. Run it once before your first online
session and once more whenever a new player joins. It takes five minutes and saves the
half-hour of "can you hear me now" that kills an online group's energy.

### The Virtual Table Checklist

Run before an online session. Printable copy in the download pack (last page).

```
BEFORE THE SESSION
   ⃝ Everyone on headphones
   ⃝ One-round sound check ("say something")
   ⃝ Shared screen ready: map / image / scene doc
   ⃝ Dice: physical on camera, or one agreed roller
   ⃝ A backup text chat, in case a mic dies

DURING
   ⃝ Initiative order posted where all can see
   ⃝ "You're up, ___ on deck" each turn
   ⃝ Go around by name each scene
   ⃝ Rescue the talked-over player

IF THE TECH BREAKS
   Fall back to theater of the mind and keep going.
   Never stop the story to fix software mid-scene.
```

Online or in person, the game is the same. Next you'll hand out treasure without wrecking
the balance you worked to build.


## Chapter 13: Treasure and Magic Items Without Breaking the Game

Handing out treasure feels like it should be the easy, fun part, and then a new DM gives a
level-one party a flaming sword and spends the next year watching every fight end in one
round. Reward is a lever, the same as an encounter is a lever, and it is as easy to
push too hard. This chapter keeps your generosity from wrecking the balance you built in
Chapter 7, while still letting the players feel rich.

The trick is simple. Give money and useful gear freely. Give raw power slowly and on
purpose. A party can never have too many interesting objects. It can easily have too
many ways to kill things without trying.

### Coins are the safe reward

Money is the reward you can hand out with both hands. It buys gear, pays for rooms and
bribes, and funds the next adventure, and none of it warps a fight. When you are not sure
how much to give, give coin and mundane gear: a better shield, a healer's kit, a horse, a
map. The players feel the win, the math of your encounters does not move, and they get to
make choices about what to buy, which is its own kind of fun.

Tie coin to the fiction so it never feels like a slot machine. The bandit captain has the
village's stolen tax box. The dead merchant's ring is worth a month's wages. Rewards that
come from the story land harder than a pile of gold that appears because the players won.

### Magic items are power, so meter them

A magic item changes what a character can do, and that is why they are precious and why
they are dangerous in a new DM's hands. Two rules keep them safe. Give one real
combat-boosting item per character across the early levels, not per session. And prefer
items that open new options over items that add raw damage. A rope that ties itself, boots
that walk on water, a cloak that hides a face: these create stories and solve problems
without turning every fight into a slaughter. Save the flaming sword for a milestone, and
it will feel earned instead of routine.

When a player does get a powerful item, let the world notice. The sword that cuts through
stone is the kind of thing other people want. Power that draws attention stays exciting,
because owning it costs something. An item nobody reacts to is only a bigger number.

### Cursed and quirky, used lightly

A little unpredictability makes treasure memorable, but a mean surprise sours a table fast.
Keep quirks flavorful, not punishing. A helm that hums when danger is near and also gives
its wearer strange dreams is fun. A helm that secretly halves the wearer's healing is a
trap that teaches players never to touch your treasure again. When in doubt, make the
downside a story hook, not a stat penalty.

> **At the table:** when you hand over a magic item, describe what it feels like, not what
> it does. "The ring is warm, and warmer when you lie" invites the player to discover its
> power in play, which is far more memorable than reading them a rules block across the
> table.

### At a real table

Omar wants to reward his level-two party after a hard dungeon. His instinct is a magic
weapon for everyone. Instead he gives them the villain's coin box, a deed to the ruined
mill, and one magic item for the whole party to argue over: a lantern that reveals hidden
doors. The coin lets them re-equip, the deed becomes a base and a plot hook, and the
lantern sends them looking for secret passages for months. He gave them a great haul and
did not touch the balance of a single future fight.

## Appendix: The Drowned Bell (A Complete First Adventure)

You have the methods. Here is a whole first session built with them, ready to run. Every
template in this book is filled in below, so you can read it as a finished adventure and
as a worked example at the same time. Change any name that does not fit your table. Cross
out anything you do not use. It is built for four new players at level one and runs in a
single evening of about three hours.

Read it once, keep this appendix open beside your screen, and go.

### The pitch, in three sentences

The village of Hollowmere sits on the edge of a flooded quarry. For a hundred years a
bell has hung in the old chapel tower at the water's bottom, silent. Three nights ago it
began to ring on its own, and every night since, someone in the village has walked into
the water in their sleep and not come back.

That is the whole hook. A ringing bell, sleepwalkers drowning, a village that is scared.
The players are strangers passing through who are asked, or paid, to make it stop.

### The opening scene (do not skip this)

Start in the flooded street at dusk, not the tavern. The party arrives as villagers are
tying a rope around a sleepwalking woman before she reaches the water. She is calm, eyes
open, humming a tune in time with a bell only she can hear. A man begs the party for
help. The trouble is already happening on the first line. That is your start.

### The First-Session Checklist, filled in

```
FIRST SESSION: The Drowned Bell        DATE: ____

1 · THE OPENING SCENE
   Where they start: the flooded main street of Hollowmere at dusk
   The trouble already happening: villagers restraining a sleepwalker
   First line I say: "She's humming. Her feet are already in the water."

2 · TONIGHT'S GOAL
   One thing they could do: find why the bell rings and silence it

3 · THREE NPCs
   Elder Maren — the reeve, wants the deaths hidden to avoid panic
   Toma — a boy whose sister already drowned, wants revenge, knows the water
   Sister Vey — a hedge-priest, wants the chapel's sin finally confessed

4 · ONE DANGER
   What can go wrong: the drowned dead rise when the bell rings
   Where/when it hits: at the bottom of the quarry, near the tower

5 · MY SAFETY LINE
   If I freeze, I ask: "What does your character do?"
```

### The village and its people

Hollowmere is thirty houses, a chapel, and a quarry that flooded a lifetime ago. The
people are tired and frightened, and each of the three named NPCs holds one of the three
clues the party needs. Play them with the one-line cards below.

```
NPC CARD — Elder Maren
   Where met: the main street, organizing the rope
   WANT: keep the deaths quiet so the village does not empty out
   TRAIT: answers every question with a question
   VOICE: clipped, official
   OPINION OF PARTY: distrusts (outsiders mean gossip)
   SECRET/HOOK: knows the drowned all shared one ancestor

NPC CARD — Toma
   Where met: at the water's edge, throwing stones at the quarry
   WANT: someone to blame, and to save the next sleeper
   TRAIT: will not look at the water while he talks about it
   VOICE: fast, angry, young
   HOOK: saw his sister walk in; she was humming the bell's tune

NPC CARD — Sister Vey
   Where met: the chapel, praying over a list of names
   WANT: the village to finally admit what happened here
   TRAIT: touches the old bell-rope burn scar on her palm
   VOICE: slow, gentle, certain
   HOOK: knows a bell-ringer was sealed in the tower alive, long ago
```

### The mystery and the three ways in

The key fact the players must reach is simple: a hundred years ago the village sealed a
plague-sick bell-ringer inside the tower to keep the sickness out, and let the quarry
flood over him. His anger is what rings the bell and calls the sleepers to join him. The
players do not need to guess this. Three separate clues each point at it, and finding any
one is enough.

```
ADVENTURE ONE-PAGE — The Drowned Bell

THE PARTY'S GOAL: stop the bell and the drownings

THREE-CLUE RULE (key fact: a ringing bell = a man sealed to drown, calling company)
   Clue path 1: Maren admits the drowned all share one ancestor, the family
                that sealed the tower
   Clue path 2: Toma repeats the tune his sister hummed; Vey knows it as the
                old ringer's hymn
   Clue path 3: church records in the chapel name a ringer "given to the water"
                in the plague year

THE CLOCK (why idling costs them)
   The bell rings again at midnight. Each night, one more villager walks in.
   Tonight it will be Toma unless they act.

PRESSURE MOVES (use if they stall)
   • The bell begins to ring early; a sleeper they know starts humming
   • Maren tries to send them away to protect the village's name

CLIFFHANGER IF YOU SPLIT IT OVER TWO NIGHTS:
   the water in the quarry begins, slowly, to lower on its own
```

### Into the water: the Drowned Bell tower

When the party goes down into the flooded quarry (a diving charm, a held breath and a
Constitution check, or "the water pulls back at midnight and a stair appears," your
call), they enter the tower as a five-room crawl.

```
5-ROOM DUNGEON — The Drowned Bell        THEME: a flooded chapel tower

WHY THE PARTY COMES HERE: to reach and silence the bell

1 · ENTRANCE + GUARDIAN
   The tower door, held shut by the weight of a hundred years of silt.
   Cost to enter: a Strength check, or clear the silt while something
   watches from the dark water.

2 · PUZZLE / ROLEPLAY CHALLENGE
   A ring of drowned villagers, calm and humming, block the stair. They will
   not fight. They will only move if a living person hums the ringer's hymn
   back to them (a clue from Toma or Vey), acknowledging him.

3 · THE SETBACK
   The bell rings. The recently drowned rise as grasping dead (see encounter
   below). The stair begins to flood again; the party has a few rounds.

4 · THE CLIMAX
   The bell-chamber. The ringer's bound spirit hangs in the water, ringing the
   bell with no hands. He wants one thing: to be heard, and freed, or destroyed.

5 · REWARD + TWIST
   Silence the bell and the water stills. The reward is the village's thanks
   and the ringer's old silver seal. The twist: the seal bears Maren's family
   crest. Her ancestors sealed him. She knew.
```

### The one fight, built to the budget

The adventure needs only one real fight, at the setback. Keep it fair for level-one
newcomers with the budget below.

```
ENCOUNTER BALANCE — The Rising Drowned        PARTY: 4 at level 1

BUDGET: standard = 4 threat points
SPEND IT:
   Drowned villager (weak, grasping) x4 ...... ~2 pts   "zombie-lite"
   Bell-called revenant (mid threat) x1 ...... ~2 pts   the loudest sleeper
   Total ≈ 4 pts (matches budget)

DANGER DIAL (turned):
   Terrain: rising water, difficult to move each round
   Reinforcements: one more drowned rises each round the bell still rings
   The way out: silence the bell and every drowned goes still at once

WHAT THEY GET FOR WINNING: the water stills, the village is saved, the seal
```

The design teaches the players the win condition through the fight: attacking the dead is
slow and endless, but the bell is the source. A smart party stops fighting and goes for
the bell. Reward that. If they do not see it, have a drowned villager, in the ringer's
voice, whisper "make it stop" and glance at the bell.

### How it ends, and where it goes next

If the party frees or destroys the ringer, the bell falls silent and the quarry stills.
Give them the village's real gratitude, a warm meal, and the silver seal. Then let the
twist land in its own time: the seal is Maren's family crest, and she has known the truth
all along. That is your hook into session two, already built by the ending of session one,
exactly as Chapter 11 promised. The players will want to know what else the village buried,
and you will run the one-hour prep on wherever they decide to look.

You have run a whole adventure out of five filled-in templates. That is the entire method
of this book, start to finish. Now go build your own.

### How to read a stat line

Each entry gives you five things and nothing you will not use in the first hour.

Threat is the point value from Chapter 7. One point is roughly a fair share of a
standard fight for one player at the monster's tier. HP is how much damage it takes to
drop. AC (armor class) is the number a player must meet or beat to hit it. Attack is the
bonus you add to a d20 when it swings, and the damage it deals on a hit. Move is the one
tactic that makes it feel like itself.

When you build a fight, add up threat to match your budget, then read the Move line so the
monsters do not all stand still and trade blows. That one line is the difference between a
memorable fight and a math problem.

Run a boss by giving it one normal turn plus one Legendary move on someone else's turn.

### A quick word on traps

Traps are monsters that do not move. Give each one a way to spot it (a check, or a clue you
narrate), a way to beat it (a check, or a clever action), and a cost for failing (damage, an
alarm, a separation). If a trap can only be found by one lucky roll and kills on a failure,
it is not a challenge, it is a coin flip. Telegraph the danger, offer more than one way past,
and let the players feel clever for beating it.

### Reskin, do not rebuild

The fastest bestiary trick is paint. The wolf, the dire wolf, and the giant boar are all
"a fast thing that knocks you down," and you can call them wolves in a forest, hounds in a
crypt, or a stampede on the road without changing a number. When you need a monster you do
not have, find the closest line above, keep the numbers, and change the name and the one
Move detail. Your players meet a fresh creature. You did no extra work.

### A note on death

New DMs fear killing a character to a bad roll, and they should. Two habits keep the game
fair. First, telegraph deadly threats: describe the ogre's reach and the drop behind the
party before the swing, so choices are informed. Second, let most defeated foes take
prisoners, demand surrender, or flee, so a lost fight is a setback and not a
funeral. Save lethal, no-mercy combat for a moment you built toward, and it will hit far
harder for being rare.

## Appendix: The Hunger Under the Hill (A Second Adventure)

Here is a second complete adventure, ready to run, pitched a little higher than the first.
The Drowned Bell suits a brand-new group at level one. The Hunger Under the Hill fits a
party of four at level two or three, once everyone has a session or two of confidence. Like
the first, every template is filled in below, so you can run it tonight or read it as a
worked example of the whole method at a slightly larger scale.

### The pitch, in three sentences

The village of Ardwick is starving through a hard winter, and a week ago the children began
to sneak away toward the old barrow hill at the edge of the fields. Those who follow them
find nothing but frost and a door in the earth that was not there before. Something under
the hill is promising the children a feast, and every night it takes a few more.

The players arrive hungry and cold to a village that has stopped hoping. Whether they came
for shelter, for pay, or by accident, Ardwick will beg them to bring the children back.

### The opening scene

Start at the barrow door at dusk, not in the village square. The party crests the hill to
find three children walking calmly toward a black opening in the frozen earth, and a mother
screaming behind a frost that will not let her follow. One child turns, smiles, and says,
"There is bread inside. Warm bread." Then he steps into the dark. The trouble is already
underway. That is your first line.

### The First-Session Checklist, filled in

```
FIRST SESSION: The Hunger Under the Hill        DATE: ____

1 · THE OPENING SCENE
   Where they start: the barrow hill at dusk, children walking to the door
   The trouble already happening: three children entering the earth
   First line I say: "He smiles and says: there is bread inside. Warm bread."

2 · TONIGHT'S GOAL
   One thing they could do: enter the barrow and bring the children back

3 · THREE NPCs
   Reeve Alder — leads Ardwick, wants the children back and the truth buried
   Old Hesper — a starving hermit, wants to confess what the village fed the hill before
   The Provider — the thing under the hill, wants to be fed, and adored

4 · ONE DANGER
   What can go wrong: the barrow's servants take a character who eats its food
   Where/when it hits: the feast-hall at the heart of the barrow

5 · MY SAFETY LINE
   If I freeze, I ask: "What does your character do?"
```

### Ardwick and its people

Ardwick is a farming village that has run out of food and out of luck. Fear has made its
people quiet and its reeve secretive, because the village has a debt to the hill that no
one wants spoken. Each named NPC carries one clue.

```
NPC CARD — Reeve Alder
   Where met: at the barrow, holding back the grieving parents
   WANT: the children back, and the old bargain kept secret
   TRAIT: will not say the word "barrow," calls it "the hill"
   VOICE: careful, official, tired
   OPINION OF PARTY: wary; useful outsiders, and possible witnesses
   SECRET: generations ago Ardwick fed the hill in lean years, and stopped

NPC CARD — Old Hesper
   Where met: a frost-rimed hut past the last field
   WANT: to unburden the truth before he starves
   TRAIT: laughs at the wrong moments, has not eaten in days
   VOICE: cracked, wandering, sudden
   HOOK: remembers his own brother walking into the hill, decades ago

NPC CARD — The Provider
   Where met: the feast-hall, at the barrow's heart
   WANT: to be fed and worshipped as it once was
   TRAIT: speaks in the voices of the children it has taken
   VOICE: warm, generous, wrong
   HOOK: it is bound to the hill and cannot leave, only lure
```

### The mystery and the three ways in

The key fact the players must reach: the thing under the hill is an old bound spirit that
Ardwick once fed in exchange for good harvests, and it is calling in a debt the village
stopped paying. Its food is a trap. Any living person who eats at its table becomes one of
its servants, and the children are being fattened for exactly that. Three clues point here.

```
ADVENTURE ONE-PAGE — The Hunger Under the Hill

THE PARTY'S GOAL: bring the children out of the barrow alive

THREE-CLUE RULE (key fact: the hill's food binds the eater; the children are being fed)
   Clue path 1: Hesper's confession about the old bargain and his lost brother
   Clue path 2: a servant of the hill still wears a decades-old face, not a child's
   Clue path 3: the feast is endless and the eaters never leave the table

THE CLOCK (why idling costs them)
   Each night, the hill takes more. On the third night, the eldest child eats,
   and eating is the point of no easy return.

PRESSURE MOVES (use if they stall)
   • A child they were told to protect slips out of the village toward the hill
   • The Provider offers the party a warm meal, kindly, at the worst moment

CLIFFHANGER IF YOU SPLIT IT:
   the party finds one rescued child humming, and asking, quietly, for bread
```

### Into the earth: the barrow

The barrow is a five-room crawl, and it is warmer and stranger the deeper it goes, because
the Provider dresses its trap as comfort.

```
5-ROOM DUNGEON — The Hunger Under the Hill        THEME: a false feast in a cold barrow

WHY THE PARTY COMES HERE: to find and free the children

1 · ENTRANCE + GUARDIAN
   A door of frozen earth that only opens to the hungry. Cost to enter:
   admit aloud that you are hungry, or force it with a Strength check as the
   frost bites (cold damage on a failure).

2 · PUZZLE / ROLEPLAY CHALLENGE
   A hall of tables piled with warm, perfect food and seated, silent eaters.
   The way through is to pass the feast without eating. Tempt the players.
   A character who eats is marked (disadvantage until they leave the hill).

3 · THE SETBACK
   The eaters rise as servants of the hill and block the way back (see the
   encounter below). The children are close ahead, being led to the head table.

4 · THE CLIMAX
   The feast-hall. The Provider, wearing the children's voices, offers the party
   a seat and a bargain: leave the children, and Ardwick will never hunger again.

5 · REWARD + TWIST
   Refuse or break the Provider, and the food turns to frost and dust. The
   children wake, thin and frightened. The reward is their lives and Ardwick's
   thanks. The twist: the Provider's last words, in Reeve Alder's voice, say
   "you promised." Alder made a new bargain to save the harvest, and gave the
   hill the children to seal it.
```

### The one fight, built to the budget

The barrow needs one real fight, at the setback, tuned for a level two or three party.

```
ENCOUNTER BALANCE — The Risen Feast        PARTY: 4 at level 2-3

BUDGET: standard = 4 threat points
SPEND IT:
   Hill-servant (former villager, grasping) x4 ... ~2 pts   "ghoul-lite, no bite save"
   Feast-warden (mid threat, herds the party) x1 ... ~2 pts  drags eaters toward the table
   Total ≈ 4 pts (matches budget)

DANGER DIAL (turned):
   Terrain: long feast-tables to climb over or shove enemies across
   Reinforcements: a seated eater wakes each round the party lingers
   The way out: reach the children, or break the feast, and the servants slow

WHAT THEY GET FOR WINNING: a clear path to the feast-hall and the climax
```

The fight teaches the lesson the adventure runs on: you cannot win by killing every
servant, because the hill makes more. The way out is forward, to the children and the
Provider, not backward through an endless feast. Reward the party that figures that out.

### How it ends, and where it goes next

Break or refuse the Provider and the barrow dies around the party as they carry the
children into the cold air. Give Ardwick its ragged, real gratitude. Then let the twist do
its work: the Provider spoke in Reeve Alder's voice, and said "you promised." The players
will want to know what Alder traded and why, and whether the hill is truly dead or only
sleeping. That is your next session, built by this one's ending, exactly as Chapter 11
promised.

You now have two complete adventures in your kit, at two difficulty levels, plus the seeds
and tables to build a dozen more. That is a campaign's worth of play from one book. Go run
it.


## Appendix: The Rules You'll Actually Use

This is not a tutorial. It is the reference card you keep at your elbow, the handful of
numbers and lists you reach for over and over while running a game. The full rules live in
the core books. What follows is the ten percent of them you touch in ninety percent of your
sessions, gathered in one place so you never stop the game to go looking.

Copy these pages, or print them from the download pack, and keep them under your screen next
to the Quick-Reference from the back of the book.

### The six abilities, and when to call each

When a player tries something uncertain, you ask for a check. This is the map from "what
they are doing" to "which ability," the same one from your ruling sheet in Chapter 4.

```
Strength ...... force, lifting, breaking, grappling, climbing under load
Dexterity ..... balance, stealth, sleight of hand, dodging, fine control
Constitution .. holding your breath, resisting poison, enduring pain and cold
Intelligence .. recalling lore, investigating clues, understanding a puzzle
Wisdom ........ noticing, sensing a lie, tracking, reading a mood, willpower
Charisma ...... persuading, deceiving, intimidating, performing, leading
```

### Difficulty, at a glance

Set the number the player must meet or beat with a d20 plus their bonus. When in doubt,
15. You do not need more precision than this.

```
Very easy ..... 5     Easy .......... 10
Medium ........ 15    Hard .......... 20
Very hard ..... 25    Near impossible 30
```

### What a character can do in a combat turn

On their turn, a character can move and take one action. Knowing the common actions cold
keeps combat fast, so you are not paging through a book while five people wait.

```
COMMON ACTIONS
   Attack ........ make one melee or ranged attack (more at higher levels)
   Cast a Spell .. most spells take one action
   Dash .......... move again, double your distance
   Disengage ..... move without provoking a free hit
   Dodge ......... attackers have disadvantage against you
   Help .......... give an ally advantage on their next try
   Hide .......... make a Dexterity (Stealth) check
   Use an Object . pull a lever, drink a potion, throw a torch

ALSO ON YOUR TURN
   A "bonus action" if something grants one, and free talking.
   One "reaction" per round, even on others' turns (like an opportunity attack
   when a foe leaves your reach).
```

### Advantage and disadvantage

The game's simplest and most useful tool. When the situation clearly helps, give advantage:
roll two d20 and keep the higher. When it clearly hurts, disadvantage: roll two and keep the
lower. Reach for this instead of inventing bonuses. It is fast, it feels good, and it covers
most of the rulings you will make.

### The conditions you will actually see

When a spell or effect imposes a condition, this is the short version of what it does. You
will use the top handful constantly and the rest rarely.

```
Prone ......... movement costs double to stand; melee attackers get advantage
Grappled ...... speed 0; ends if the grappler is knocked away
Restrained .... speed 0; attacks against it have advantage; it attacks with disadvantage
Poisoned ...... disadvantage on attacks and ability checks
Frightened .... disadvantage while the source is in sight; cannot move closer to it
Blinded ....... cannot see; attacks against it have advantage, its own have disadvantage
Stunned ....... cannot act; attacks against it have advantage
Unconscious ... drops everything, prone; attackers have advantage, and crit in melee
```

### Resting, the short version

Two kinds of rest let characters recover, and knowing the difference sets the pace of your
adventuring day. A short rest is about an hour: characters can spend resources to heal some
and recover certain abilities. A long rest is about eight hours of sleep: they regain most
of their health and their spent abilities. As a rule of thumb, aim for a couple of fights
between long rests, so resources matter and the day has a shape.

### Death, the short version

When a character drops to zero, they are not dead yet. They make death saving throws on
their turns: a 10 or higher succeeds, lower fails, three successes stabilize, three failures
end them, and a natural 20 brings them back with one hit point. Any healing pulls them back
to their feet. This buffer is on purpose. It gives the table a tense moment and their
friends a chance to act, instead of a single roll deciding a character's story.

### Keep this near

None of this replaces reading the core rules once. It replaces looking things up mid-scene,
which is where new DMs lose the room. Glance here, make the call, keep the game moving. After
a dozen sessions you will not need the card, because it will be in your head, put there by
the only teacher that works: playing.
